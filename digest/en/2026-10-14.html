Batch size and direction-dependent optimization dynamics

The Best Optimizer Depends on Batch Size ↗
Xingyu Dang · Kaiyue Wen · Sadhika Malladi
First posted Oct 6 · v2 Oct 12

With a fixed sample budget TTT, a larger batch size BBB reduces gradient noise but also reduces the number of updates. The paper separates these effects using a noisy quadratic model.[1]

Mechanism and scope

Different directions can occupy different regimes at once, so a single scaling exponent cannot accommodate all of them. This is a local explanation at a fixed position with stationary momentum; it does not preserve the entire training trajectory.[1][2]

Directional intervention. Starting from the same checkpoint, increase the batch from 128K128\mathrm K128K to 2M2\mathrm M2M, retaining small-batch updates only in selected directions. The recovered fraction of the local loss gap is

Experimental scope

Most branches are single runs, and the effect weakens substantially late in training. The 59.5% figure measures recovery of a local loss gap; it is not a final-loss or training-cost improvement. Changes from v1 to v2 have not been checked individually.[2]

Noise can smooth the sign—and change the order of feature learning

From Parallel to Sequential: Small-Batch Noise Restores Simplicity Bias of Muon ↗
Tom Jacobs · Rebekka Burkholz
arXiv Oct 12 · OpenReview record dated Oct 1

The authors report that small-batch noise restores sequential feature learning absent from full-batch spectral descent, and formalize the mechanism in deep linear models.[3][4]

The following illustrative scalar derivation explains noise smoothing. For z∼N(0,1)z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)z∼N(0,1) and σ>0\sigma>0σ>0,

Scalar intuition
E[sign⁡(g+σz)]=2Φ(g/σ)−1=2πgσ+O ⁣((g/σ)3),∣g∣≪σ.\begin{aligned}\mathbb E[\operatorname{sign}(g+\sigma z)]&=2\Phi(g/\sigma)-1\\&=\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\frac g\sigma+O\!\left((g/\sigma)^3\right),\qquad |g|\ll\sigma.\end{aligned}E[sign(g+σz)]​=2Φ(g/σ)−1=π2​​σg​+O((g/σ)3),∣g∣≪σ.​
Evidence boundary

For weak signals, the mean sign update retains gradient magnitude. This scalar calculation does not replace the paper’s matrix theorem; its full theoretical assumptions have not been verified here.

Discovering a scaling law has its own compute budget

SLDBench v2: Active Scaling Law Discovery ↗
Lingbei Meng et al.
First posted Oct 12
Background

Chinchilla (2022) used small-scale training runs to study compute allocation between model size and data. SLDBench (2025; ICLR 2026) then asked agents to discover predictive laws automatically. The new question: obtaining the fitting data itself costs compute.[6][7]

Candidate law
L(N,D)=E+aN−α+bD−β.L(N,D)=E+aN^{-\alpha}+bD^{-\beta}.L(N,D)=E+aN−α+bD−β.

The expression above is only a candidate form. The agent neither knows the correct functional form nor gets all observations for free; it receives a catalog of priced experiments.[5]

Experiment catalog
U={(xi,ci)}i=1n,ci>0.\mathcal U=\{(x_i,c_i)\}_{i=1}^{n},\qquad c_i>0.U={(xi​,ci​)}i=1n​,ci​>0.

xix_ixi​ is a training configuration; cic_ici​ is usually its training FLOPs. The outcome yiy_iyi​ is revealed only after purchase. Omitting group indices, the agent buys AtA_tAt​ each round and stops at time τ\tauτ:

Spending
St+1=St∪At,ρ=∑i∈Sτci∑i∈Uci.S_{t+1}=S_t\cup A_t,\qquad \rho=\frac{\sum_{i\in S_\tau}c_i}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal U}c_i}.St+1​=St​∪At​,ρ=∑i∈U​ci​∑i∈Sτ​​ci​​.

Evaluation records (extrapolation error at larger scales, ρ)(\text{extrapolation error at larger scales},\,\rho)(extrapolation error at larger scales,ρ). The agent must jointly decide which experiments to buy, when to stop, and which law to fit. Test configurations are larger than candidate experiments and cannot be purchased directly.[5]

What to take away

Cheap experiments may cover every group while losing the independent parameter variation needed to identify the law. Experimental design adds a bottleneck to automated scaling-law discovery; these results concern the selected difficult tasks.[5]

S4: one state space, three ways to compute

Efficiently Modeling Long Sequences with Structured State Spaces ↗
Albert Gu · Karan Goel · Christopher Ré
First posted Oct 2021 · ICLR 2022 · reading v3 (Aug 2022)
ICLR 2022 Oral · Outstanding Paper Honorable Mention ↗
Where it comes from

Long sequences require both memory and efficient training. HiPPO (2020) designed continuous memory through polynomial projections; S4 (2022) sought a state-space parameterization that is fast to compute and numerically stable.[10][13]

Consider one channel with scalar input and output, and hidden state x(t)∈RNx(t)\in\mathbb R^Nx(t)∈RN. AAA governs memory evolution, BBB writes the input, and CCC reads the state. Omit the separately computable skip term.[12]

Continuous system
x˙(t)=Ax(t)+Bu(t),y(t)=Cx(t).\dot x(t)=Ax(t)+Bu(t),\qquad y(t)=Cx(t).x˙(t)=Ax(t)+Bu(t),y(t)=Cx(t).

Apply a bilinear discretization with step size Δ>0\Delta>0Δ>0, assuming the inverse exists. Δ\DeltaΔ is the sampling timescale and can be learned as a model parameter.[12]

Discretization
Aˉ=(I−Δ2A)−1(I+Δ2A),Bˉ=(I−Δ2A)−1ΔB.\bar A=\left(I-\frac{\Delta}{2}A\right)^{-1}\left(I+\frac{\Delta}{2}A\right),\qquad \bar B=\left(I-\frac{\Delta}{2}A\right)^{-1}\Delta B.Aˉ=(I−2Δ​A)−1(I+2Δ​A),Bˉ=(I−2Δ​A)−1ΔB.

With zero initial state x−1=0x_{-1}=0x−1​=0 and fixed parameters, stepwise recurrence and full-sequence convolution are exactly equivalent:[12]

RNN ↔ convolution
xk=Aˉxk−1+Bˉuk,yk=∑j=0kKjuk−j,Kj=CAˉjBˉ.\begin{aligned}x_k&=\bar A x_{k-1}+\bar B u_k,\\ y_k&=\sum_{j=0}^{k}K_j u_{k-j},\qquad K_j=C\bar A^j\bar B.\end{aligned}xk​yk​​=Aˉxk−1​+Bˉuk​,=j=0∑k​Kj​uk−j​,Kj​=CAˉjBˉ.​
Two execution modes for the same layer

Use convolution to process the full sequence in parallel during training; retain only the current state during autoregressive generation. Linearity applies to the individual SSM kernel. Channel mixing and nonlinearities between layers give the full network nonlinear expressive power.[10][12]

Computing the kernel is the real bottleneck. Direct eigendecomposition of a HiPPO matrix can be severely ill-conditioned. S4 uses a normal matrix plus a low-rank correction, then a unitary change of basis gives DPLR:[10]

Structured parameterization
A=V(Λ−PQ∗)V∗,V∗V=I,P,Q∈CN×r,r≪N.A=V(\Lambda-PQ^*)V^*,\qquad V^*V=I,\qquad P,Q\in\mathbb C^{N\times r},\quad r\ll N.A=V(Λ−PQ∗)V∗,V∗V=I,P,Q∈CN×r,r≪N.

Λ\LambdaΛ is diagonal. Write A^=Λ−PQ∗\widehat A=\Lambda-PQ^*A=Λ−PQ∗ and R(ω)=(ωI−Λ)−1R(\omega)=(\omega I-\Lambda)^{-1}R(ω)=(ωI−Λ)−1. When the relevant inverses exist, Woodbury’s identity gives:[10]

Low-rank correction
(ωI−A^)−1=R−RP(I+Q∗RP)−1Q∗R.(\omega I-\widehat A)^{-1}=R-RP(I+Q^*RP)^{-1}Q^*R.(ωI−A)−1=R−RP(I+Q∗RP)−1Q∗R.

A high-dimensional inverse becomes a diagonal inverse plus an r×rr\times rr×r correction. The paper reduces the finite-length kernel’s generating function to Cauchy computations and an inverse FFT. For fixed low rank, theoretical kernel construction costs O~(N+L)\widetilde O(N+L)O(N+L), and each recurrent step costs O(N)O(N)O(N). These are per-SSM costs and assume a fast Cauchy algorithm.[10]

Discussion · our interpretation

Kj=CAˉjBˉK_j=C\bar A^j\bar BKj​=CAˉjBˉ suggests that long memory depends on both dynamics and input–output coupling. Is placing eigenvalues near the unit circle enough? For non-normal Aˉ\bar AAˉ, which transient effects does the spectral radius miss? These are questions for further discussion, not conclusions proved by the paper.
