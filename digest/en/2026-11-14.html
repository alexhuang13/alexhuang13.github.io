Recovering second moments with input-side rotation

Factored One-sided Adam-Moment for Practical and Scalable SOAP ↗
Jaemyung Yu · Byeongho Heo · Sangdoo Yun · Dongyoon Han
v1 · 2026-11-12 09:59 EST

SOAP (2024) maintains rotations on both sides of the gradient matrix and runs Adam in the rotated basis. FOAM keeps the input eigenvectors Q and uses row and column statistics in the spirit of Adafactor (2018). Let G be the m×n gradient matrix, with input and output second-moment factors R and L. [1]

Lemma 1; assumes K-FAC separability and column-major vectorization
E[vec⁡(G)vec⁡(G)⊤]=R⊗L,Q⊤RQ=diag⁡(λ)⇒E[(GQ)ij2]=Liiλj\mathbb E[\operatorname{vec}(G)\operatorname{vec}(G)^\top]=R\otimes L,\quad Q^\top RQ=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)\quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathbb E[(GQ)_{ij}^2]=L_{ii}\lambda_jE[vec(G)vec(G)⊤]=R⊗L,Q⊤RQ=diag(λ)⇒E[(GQ)ij2​]=Lii​λj​
Eq. (6); bias-corrected EMAs of row and column means, with reconstructed mass fixed at tr(R)
V^ij=tr⁡(R)∥r^∥1∥c^∥1r^ic^j\widehat V_{ij}=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(R)}{\|\widehat r\|_1\|\widehat c\|_1}\widehat r_i\widehat c_jVij​=∥r∥1​∥c∥1​tr(R)​ri​cj​

Experiments tune each optimizer at 124M, 350M, and 760M. The table uses the 760M model, 15.49B FineWeb tokens, and 8×H100. Final losses are three-seed means ± standard deviations. Time to target uses the same validation-loss threshold of 2.847 in every row. [1]

Evidence boundary

Under the factorization, the population second moment sums to tr(R)tr(L). Eq. (6) therefore recovers the rank-one shape in the population; the written normalization requires tr(L)=1 for the absolute scale. Measured times depend on the training parallelization. [1]

Replacing multilingual data from a fixed checkpoint

Lara Mini Base: Language Diversity as a Resource, Not a Trade-off ↗
Translated team
v1 · 2026-11-12 10:28 EST

Translated’s MoE base has 7.2B total and 0.6B active parameters and trains on roughly 6T tokens. From a 5.4T checkpoint, the authors run two 100B-token anneals, replacing multilingual data with English in one branch. Writing scores as a function of mixture weights makes the direction of substitution explicit. [2]

Notation for these notes; coordinates are English, other languages, and code/math/SFT-like data; j indexes benchmark groups
p=(.27,.33,.40),p′=(.60,0,.40),ΔSj=Sj(p′;θ5.4T)−Sj(p;θ5.4T)p=(.27,.33,.40),\quad p^\prime=(.60,0,.40),\quad \Delta S_j=S_j(p^\prime;\theta_{5.4\mathrm{T}})-S_j(p;\theta_{5.4\mathrm{T}})p=(.27,.33,.40),p′=(.60,0,.40),ΔSj​=Sj​(p′;θ5.4T​)−Sj​(p;θ5.4T​)

Separate single-run, 100B-token ablations compare optimizers around 0.5B active parameters. Reading the curves at matched training loss, the authors report that AdamW needs 1.5–1.9 times as many tokens as Muon. [2]

Evidence boundary

The mixture comparison covers one late checkpoint and one substitution direction. Full-run mixture effects, the separate contributions of routing and data selection, and end-to-end time at matched quality remain unresolved. [2]

Turning written principles into preference supervision

Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback ↗
Yuntao Bai · Saurav Kadavath · Sandipan Kundu · Amanda Askell · et al.
First posted 2022-12-15 ET; read v1
Semantic Scholar · 4,021 citations (checked 2026-11-14) ↗

The 2022 helpful-and-harmless RLHF work trains a preference model from human comparisons. Constitutional AI splits harmlessness supervision into two decisions: have a model revise answers using written principles, then have an independent feedback model compare responses under a principle. Helpfulness labels still come from humans. [7][9]

Notation for §3.1. x is a red-team prompt, aₜ is the sampled principle, cₜ is a critique, and yₜ is the revision
y0∼πhelpful(⋅∣x),ct∼q(⋅∣x,yt−1,at),yt∼q(⋅∣x,yt−1,ct,at)y_0\sim\pi_{\rm helpful}(\cdot\mid x),\quad c_t\sim q(\cdot\mid x,y_{t-1},a_t),\quad y_t\sim q(\cdot\mid x,y_{t-1},c_t,a_t)y0​∼πhelpful​(⋅∣x),ct​∼q(⋅∣x,yt−1​,at​),yt​∼q(⋅∣x,yt−1​,ct​,at​)
Supervised learning fits revisions from all stages and includes helpful examples; the expectation samples the training mixture
LSL(θ)=−E(x,y)∼Drev∪Dhelplog⁡πθ(y∣x)\mathcal L_{\rm SL}(\theta)=-\mathbb E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal D_{\rm rev}\cup\mathcal D_{\rm help}}\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)LSL​(θ)=−E(x,y)∼Drev​∪Dhelp​​logπθ​(y∣x)
Soft labels in §4.1; ℓ is the feedback model’s log probability for each option under principle a. A score difference parameterizes the preference model
pa=eℓAeℓA+eℓB,p^ϕ=σ(rϕ(x,yA)−rϕ(x,yB))p_a=\frac{e^{\ell_A}}{e^{\ell_A}+e^{\ell_B}},\quad \widehat p_\phi=\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_A)-r_\phi(x,y_B)\big)pa​=eℓA​+eℓB​eℓA​​,p​ϕ​=σ(rϕ​(x,yA​)−rϕ​(x,yB​))
Mathematical form for the harmlessness part of preference fitting; training also uses helpfulness comparisons. RL then starts from SL-CAI
Lpair(ϕ)=−E[palog⁡p^ϕ+(1−pa)log⁡(1−p^ϕ)]\mathcal L_{\rm pair}(\phi)=-\mathbb E\big[p_a\log\widehat p_\phi+(1-p_a)\log(1-\widehat p_\phi)\big]Lpair​(ϕ)=−E[pa​logp​ϕ​+(1−pa​)log(1−p​ϕ​)]

In the human Elo comparisons in §4.3, RL-CAI scored higher on harmlessness than the RLHF and SL-CAI systems in the paper. Overconfidence in chain-of-thought feedback led the authors to clamp those soft targets to [0.4, 0.6]. Longer RL runs still produced boilerplate or overly stern responses. Elo measures relative preferences; transfer to other populations and prompt distributions requires evaluation. [7]
